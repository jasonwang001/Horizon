### [Genome language models generate viable phage genomes](https://www.reddit.com/r/MachineLearning/comments/1vjj4pr/r_generative_design_of_novel_bacteriophages_with/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Researchers applied the genome language models Evo 1 and Evo 2 to generate whole bacteriophage genome sequences, using the lytic phage ΦX174 as a design template, and experimentally confirmed that 16 of the AI-designed genomes produced viable phages. This is reported as the first generative design of viable bacteriophage genomes and demonstrates that such models can produce functional sequences at whole-genome scale with realistic genetic architectures and desirable host tropism, while yielding substantial evolutionary novelty. The work highlights a novel approach for synthetic biology and machine learning, though the resulting phages still carry considerable genetic novelty rather than being precise copies of natural designs.

reddit · r/MachineLearning · /u/moschles · Aug 9, 07:11

**「Background」** Genome language models \(gLMs\) such as Evo 1 and Evo 2 are autoregressive neural networks built on the same conceptual architecture as GPT-style text models but trained on DNA sequences rather than natural language, allowing them to learn patterns of genome organization. Earlier gLMs, including Evo and MegaDNA, were used for prokaryotic genome design at scale, but had not been shown to produce functional sequences at the scale of whole viral genomes. This work tests whether such models can move from generating realistic-looking sequences to producing viable bacteriophages in the lab.

**「Impact」** For researchers in synthetic biology and genome engineering, this proof-of-concept shows that genome language models can produce experimentally validated, viable organisms, opening a path toward AI-driven design of phages and potentially other microbial genomes.

### [Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change, a Timeless W3C Warning, Still Resonates](https://www.w3.org/Provider/Style/URI) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The W3C&\#x27;s classic 1998 essay &\#x27;Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change&\#x27; has resurfaced on Hacker News, renewing attention to the principle that URLs should remain stable permanently. Community examples show link rot still persists decades later: a Microsoft support link from Windows 10 led to a generic landing page, and an NSF publication URL returned a 404. Commenters note that SEO concerns and WordPress slug redirects have mitigated some breakage, but neglect, reorganizations, and sites going offline still destroy links. The essay itself has reportedly kept the same URI for 28 years, underscoring the value of the advice it gives.

hackernews · Klaster\_1 · Aug 9, 14:32 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49231809)

**「Background」** Cool URIs Don&\#x27;t Change is a 1998 World Wide Web Consortium \(W3C\) style essay by Tim Berners-Lee, the inventor of the Web. It argues that a well-designed URI should remain stable and unchanged, because changing a URL breaks all existing links and citations to that resource. The piece is widely cited as a foundational argument against link rot, and it remains a reference point in web architecture discussions.

**「Impact」** For web developers and engineers, the discussion is a concrete reminder that stable URLs require ongoing maintenance rather than just initial design, as even recent Microsoft and NSF links have failed.

**「Community Discussion」** Commenters generally agree the essay has aged well, but some question whether URLs can ever be truly permanent because they encode the access method, unlike abstract identifiers such as UUIDs. Others observe that CMS redirects and SEO practices have helped, yet official resources still get removed or reorganized.
